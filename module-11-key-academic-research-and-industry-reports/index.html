    11.1-11.9 Full Research Catalogue

    Market Projections

    • Approaching the Tokenization Tipping Point

      • Author/Publisher: Boston Consulting Group (BCG) & Ripple.
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Tokenized real-world assets are projected to reach $18.9 trillion by 2033 in a base scenario, with an optimistic projection of $23.4 trillion.
        • Identifies three distinct phases of adoption: low-risk pilots (e.g., money market funds), institutional adoption (higher-yield assets), and a system-level shift to illiquid asset classes.
        • The banking sector is expected to account for over 50% of all tokenized assets by the end of the decade.
      • Access: Ripple website.

    • Tokenization Market Size, Share and Trends 2026 to 2035

      • Author/Publisher: Precedence Research.
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • The global market size was valued at $4.02 billion in 2025 and is projected to hit $24.13 billion by 2035.
        • The market will expand at a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of 19.63% through 2035.
        • Growth is largely driven by the increasing demand for secure digital asset management, faster transactions, and wider blockchain adoption.
      • Access: www.precedenceresearch.com/tokenization-market.

    • Asset Tokenization Market Size, Trends, Share & Industry Report 2031

      • Author/Publisher: Mordor Intelligence.
      • Year: 2026.
      • Key Findings:
        • The asset tokenization market is projected to grow from $3.01 trillion in 2026 to $18.74 trillion by 2031.
        • The market is experiencing a 44.25% CAGR, powered by regulatory clarity and institutional capital allocation.
        • Real estate currently leads with a 30.12% market share (as of 2025), while commodities are projected to be the fastest-growing sector.
      • Access: www.mordorintelligence.com.

    • Real-World Asset Tokenization: A Game Changer for Global Trade

      • Author/Publisher: Standard Chartered and Synpulse.
      • Year: 2024.
      • Key Findings:
        • Overall tokenized real-world assets are projected to reach up to $30.1 trillion by 2034.
        • Trade finance assets are expected to be among the top three tokenized assets globally.
        • Expansion is driven by improved liquidity, faster settlement processes, and increased global access.
      • Access: hkdca.com.

    Technical Implementation

    • Exploration on Real World Assets (RWAs) & Tokenization

      • Author/Publisher: Ning Xia, Xiaolei Zhao, Yimin Yang, Yixuan Li, Yucong Li (Columbia University).
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Provides a comprehensive technical analysis of the tokenization process, covering blockchain fundamentals, consensus mechanisms, and smart contracts.
        • Examines core token standards (ERC-20, ERC-721, ERC-1155) and their practical implementation.
        • Analyzes the advantages, challenges, and regulatory considerations using real-world case studies like real estate and NFTs.
      • Access: arXiv.org (arXiv:2503.01111).

    • Tokenization of Financial Assets

      • Author/Publisher: X. Lavayssière (SSRN).
      • Year: 2023.
      • Key Findings:
        • Provides an interdisciplinary analysis of tokenization bridging financial, technical, and legal perspectives.
        • Introduces the concept of "direct tokenization" and establishes sharedness and trust-minimization as essential properties.
        • Discusses varying custodianship models and how ownership representation shifts on-chain.
      • Access: papers.ssrn.com (abstract 4649162).

    • An Ultra-Scalable Blockchain Platform for Universal Asset Tokenization: Design and Implementation

      • Author/Publisher: A. Buldas, D. Draheim, M. Gault, et al. (IEEE Access).
      • Year: 2022.
      • Key Findings:
        • Introduces unlimited framework scalability utilizing Alphabill technology.
        • Highlights the use of the highly robust KSI Cash currency alongside the Alphabill Platform to achieve hyper-fast transaction throughput.
        • Provides features facilitating cross-token transfers but notes that the underlying network requires further evaluation for ledger security and trust.
      • Access: IEEE Access (doi:10.1109/ACCESS.2022.3192837).

    Regulatory Analysis

    • The Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) and the EU Digital Finance Strategy

      • Author/Publisher: SSRN.
      • Year: 2020.
      • Key Findings:
        • Provides a comprehensive early analysis of the EU's MiCA regulation.
        • Compares the MiCA approach to existing EU financial laws to highlight regulatory shifts.
        • Discusses the supervisory roles granted to the European Banking Authority (EBA) and national authorities.
      • Access: papers.ssrn.com (abstract 3725395).

    • Towards a Harmonized Global Regulation: An Analysis of the MiCA Regulation

      • Author/Publisher: IEEE.
      • Year: 2024.
      • Key Findings:
        • Examines MiCA proposals and the impact of a standardized regulatory framework on the European crypto-asset market.
        • Analyzes the resulting impact on innovation versus consumer protection.
        • Discusses potential implementation challenges and the risks of regulatory arbitrage.
      • Access: IEEE Xplore (document 10634355).

    • Tokenization of Real-World Assets: Legal Frameworks, Market Dynamics, and Policy Pathways for a Decentralized Financial Future

      • Author/Publisher: Rajmund Mirdala (Journal of Applied Economic Sciences / RITHA Publishing).
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Analyzes global legal frameworks with a strong focus on the EU's MiCA regulation.
        • Provides detailed case studies of institutional shifts, notably the European Investment Bank's digital bonds and BlackRock's BUIDL.
        • Evaluates difficulties in implementation, particularly concerning technological compatibility and legal recognition challenges.
      • Access: RITHA EU Journals (https://doi.org/10.57017/jaes.v20.2(88).09).

    • Emerging Regulatory Approaches to Blockchain-Based Token Economy

      • Author/Publisher: Agata Ferreira (Journal of British Blockchain Association).
      • Year: 2020.
      • Key Findings:
        • Conducts a comparative analysis of global regulatory approaches to blockchain and token economies.
        • Highlights existing regulatory voids, divergent frameworks, and the risks they pose to investors and innovation.
        • Concludes there is an urgent need for clear, technology-neutral legal and regulatory frameworks to realize the token economy's potential.
      • Access: jbba.scholasticahq.com.

    • PwC Global Crypto Regulation Report 2026

      • Author/Publisher: PwC.
      • Year: 2026.
      • Key Findings:
        • Identifies significant divergence across regions (e.g., EU's MiCAR vs. US GENIUS Act/NYDFS).
        • Notes that Europe is shifting from rule-making to active supervision and delivery under MiCAR and DORA.
        • Firms will increasingly need to embed compliance by design into their operating models to scale successfully.
      • Access: pwc.de (pwc-global-crypto-regulation-report-2026.pdf).

    • Report on Tokenised Deposits (EBA/REP/2024/24)

      • Author/Publisher: European Banking Authority (EBA).
      • Year: 2024.
      • Key Findings:
        • Defines tokenised deposits and establishes indicative characteristics to distinguish them from Electronic Money Tokens (EMTs) regulated under MiCAR.
        • Concludes there is no immediate need to adjust the regulatory and supervisory framework, citing limited market presence to date.
        • Recommends carrying out further targeted analyses following a risk-based approach as empirical evidence grows.
      • Access: eba.europa.eu.

    Private Markets

    • Private Equity Tokenization: Mapping the Opportunities and Solving Confidentiality

      • Author/Publisher: Jason Delabays (Zama).
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Projects that over $4 trillion in private market assets could be tokenized by 2030.
        • Identifies regulatory fragmentation and lack of confidentiality as the main blockers to institutional adoption.
        • Discusses how Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) can be used to keep sensitive data confidential during on-chain transactions.
      • Access: www.zama.org.

    • Tokenization of Private Assets: Unlocking Liquidity, Transparency, and Access

      • Author/Publisher: Tommaso Cervellati (CAIA).
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Focuses on the tokenization of private equity, private debt, real estate, and infrastructure.
        • Highlights core benefits: enhanced liquidity, fractional ownership, and radical transparency.
        • Echoes Citi Group projections that tokenization in private markets will reach $4 trillion (or 10% of the PE/VC market) by 2030.
      • Access: caia.org.

    • How Tokenization Can Fuel a $400 Billion Opportunity in Distributing Alternative Investments to Individuals

      • Author/Publisher: J.P. Morgan (Kinexys) & Bain & Company.
      • Year: 2024/2025 (Contextual).
      • Key Findings:
        • Identifies a $400 billion full-potential revenue opportunity in distributing alternatives to individuals.
        • Notes that market participants target a 20% allocation to alternative investments.
        • Highlights that ultra-high-net-worth investors currently have 19% allocations, showing room for democratized access via tokenization.
      • Access: www.jpmorgan.com.

    • A Data-Driven Framework for Identifying Investment Opportunities in Private Equity

      • Author/Publisher: arXiv.
      • Year: 2022.
      • Key Findings:
        • Proposes a framework for the automated screening of Private Equity investments.
        • Utilizes explainable AI for providing transparent investment recommendations.
        • Demonstrates how data-driven approaches combined with tokenization can broaden access to PE markets.
      • Access: arXiv.org (arXiv:2204.01852).

    Token Standards

    • Tokenization Standards: Taming the Regulatory Menagerie

      • Author/Publisher: Nethermind & PwC Germany.
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Explores the critical intersection between technical token standards and EU regulatory compliance (MiCAR and MiFID II).
        • Provides a deep dive into ERC-20, ERC-721, ERC-1155, ERC-1400, ERC-3643, and CMTAT.
        • Concludes that compliance-aware token standards (like ERC-3643 and ERC-1400) are essential for institutional adoption of regulated financial products.
      • Access: Nethermind & PwC websites.

    • ERC-20 Token Standard

      • Author/Publisher: Fabian Vogelsteller and Vitalik Buterin (Ethereum Improvement Proposals).
      • Year: 2015.
      • Key Findings:
        • The foundational, official documentation defining the API for fungible tokens on smart contracts.
        • Outlines basic functionalities: transferring tokens, getting account balances, and allowing third-party spending approvals.
        • Remains the most widely used token standard due to its simplicity, driving ecosystem interoperability.
      • Access: ethereum.org/developers/docs/standards/tokens/erc-20/.

    • ERC-3643 vs ERC-1400: Security Token Standards Explained

      • Author/Publisher: Pedex.
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Provides a comprehensive comparison between the two leading security token standards.
        • ERC-1400 is favored for managing complex partitions and tranches, while ERC-3643 excels in automated identity-based compliance.
        • Delivers implementation guidance tailored to helping founders choose the appropriate standard based on regulatory needs.
      • Access: pedex.org.

    • Fractional Non-Fungible Tokens: Overview, Evaluation, Marketplaces, and Challenges

      • Author/Publisher: Wiley Online Library.
      • Year: 2024.
      • Key Findings:
        • Analyzes the mechanics of fractional NFTs and their role in asset tokenization.
        • Evaluates how ERC-20, ERC-721, and ERC-1155 standards interact to enable fractional ownership.
        • Examines minting processes, reconstitution challenges, and the burden of regulatory compliance.
      • Access: onlinelibrary.wiley.com (doi: 10.1002/nem.2260).

    • Real Estate Tokenization: Exploring Definitions, Mechanisms, and Future Applications

      • Author/Publisher: IEEE.
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Investigates why foundational standards like ERC-20, ERC-721, and ERC-1400 continue to dominate real estate.
        • Analyzes why newer standards (e.g., ERC-777) have seen limited adoption.
        • Emphasizes that simplicity, regulatory compatibility, and extensive developer support are the main drivers of standard selection.
      • Access: IEEE Xplore (document 11334076).

    Industry Case Studies

    • From Equity to Real Estate: The Institutionalization of Asset Tokenization in Capital Markets

      • Author/Publisher: Nikhil Sudhakar Jarunde (IJSAT).
      • Year: 2025.
      • Key Findings:
        • Dissects the legal and operational challenges of migrating asset rights from paper to cryptographically secured ledgers.
        • Evaluates smart-contract governance models, including multi-signature escrows and on-chain compliance oracles.
        • Uses a mixed-methods approach combining legal analysis with event-study evidence from pilot offerings to assess democratizing potential and algorithmic risks.
      • Access: www.ijsat.org (paper ID 7407).

    • Blockchain-Enabled Tokenization for Sustainable and Inclusive Infrastructure Investment

      • Author/Publisher: arXiv (Y. Tian et al.).
      • Year: 2022.
      • Key Findings:
        • Analyzes 21 distinct projects implementing tokenization within the energy infrastructure sector.
        • Examines how tokenization improves investment liquidity, transparency, and operational efficiency.
        • Discusses how blockchain can integrate non-financial values to promote sustainability in infrastructure.
      • Access: arXiv.org (arXiv:2208.04709).

    • Initial Crypto-Asset Offerings (ICOs), Tokenization and Corporate Governance

      • Author/Publisher: arXiv.
      • Year: 2019.
      • Key Findings:
        • Provides a historical analysis tracking the evolution of ICOs, ITOs, and early token sales.
        • Examines the severe corporate governance implications caused by decentralizing ownership via tokenization.
        • Discusses the extreme diversity of crypto-assets and resulting legal classification challenges.
      • Access: arXiv.org (arXiv:1905.03340).

    • Blockchain-Based Equity and STOs: Towards a Liquid Market for SME Financing

      • Author/Publisher: Florie Mazzorana-Kremer (Scientific Research Publishing / Theoretical Economics Letters).
      • Year: 2019.
      • Key Findings:
        • Analyzes Security Token Offerings (STOs) as a highly viable alternative funding mechanism for SMEs.
        • Compares STOs against traditional IPOs, private equity, and crowdfunding.
        • Concludes that while technical standards facilitate exchange, ultimate liquidity heavily depends on issuer quality and specialized trading platforms.
      • Access: www.scirp.org (Paper ID 93164) / Theoretical Economics Letters.

    • Tokenization and the reshaping traditional finance: institutional adoption

      • Author/Publisher: Sujay Davé, Anil Donmez, Ioannis Gkatzimas (Frontiers in Blockchain).
      • Year: 2026.
      • Key Findings:
        • Examines institutional adoption through the lens of recent high-profile launches, specifically BlackRock's BUIDL fund.
        • Outlines the precise lifecycle of a hypothetical tokenized bond, including the use of smart contracts and data oracles at each stage.
        • Analyzes how tokenization fundamentally interacts with and influences existing financial infrastructure and market structure.
      • Access: www.frontiersin.org (doi: 10.3389/fbloc.2026.1747208).

    Knowledge Check Quiz

    Here is a 5-question multiple-choice quiz designed to test an executive’s strategic understanding of key academic research and industry reports related to asset tokenization.

    Executive Quiz: Key Academic Research & Industry Reports on Tokenization

    1. According to the joint report by Boston Consulting Group (BCG) and Ripple, the tokenized asset market is projected to reach approximately $18.9 trillion by 2033. Which sector is projected to hold the largest share of these tokenized assets, and what is the strategic implication for executives?

    A) Real estate, as fractional ownership will democratize access for retail investors and outpace institutional volumes.

    B) The banking sector, which is expected to account for over 50% of all tokenized assets, highlighting the shift toward institutional adoption and wholesale market integration.

    C) The decentralized finance (DeFi) sector, capturing 75% of the market due to the displacement of traditional financial intermediaries.

    D) Public equities in advanced economies, where tokenization will replace existing highly efficient electronic book entries.

    2. The World Economic Forum (WEF) and Accenture identify specific, practical value drivers for financial institutions adopting tokenization. When structuring a business case for a new tokenized product, which of the following best represents the primary operational advantage cited by these reports?

    A) Creating entirely public and transparent transaction histories to satisfy retail investor demand for open-source data without privacy controls.

    B) Enabling "programmable trust" through smart contracts that eliminate manual reconciliation and T+2 settlement delays, drastically improving capital efficiency.

    C) Utilizing permissionless blockchains exclusively to bypass traditional regulatory perimeters and lower issuance costs.

    D) Tokenizing highly liquid public equities in advanced economies to achieve marginal cost reductions in an already hyper-efficient market.

    3. Bain & Company estimates a $400 billion annual revenue opportunity in the tokenization of alternative investments (e.g., private equity, private credit). For a platform CEO focused on distribution, which industry participant is identified as being best positioned to capture this specific opportunity and why?

    A) Public blockchain miners, because they collect the transaction fees (gas) associated with smart contract execution across alternative asset classes.

    B) Native crypto exchanges, as they possess the most advanced matching engines for 24/7 trading of illiquid assets.

    C) Wealth managers and wholesale platforms, because they possess established distribution models, extensive client relationships, and can flatten marginal asset servicing costs for new investors.

    D) Central banks, by issuing wholesale CBDCs that automatically capture the spread on secondary market transfers.

    4. While industry reports forecast massive growth, the Financial Stability Board (FSB) has highlighted several systemic vulnerabilities that executives must mitigate when scaling tokenized platforms. Which of the following is a primary risk identified by the FSB?

    A) Liquidity and maturity mismatches arising when the settlement timeline or liquidity profile of a token differs from its underlying reference asset.

    B) The inherent deflationary nature of tokenized assets causing collateral shortages in traditional repurchase (repo) agreements.

    C) The technological inability of smart contracts to handle asset fractionalization, leading to "trapped capital" in private equity funds.

    D) The mandatory requirement by global regulators that all tokenized securities must settle exclusively using volatile cryptocurrencies.

    5. McKinsey notes that while the tokenization of financial assets is technologically feasible, achieving true scale is hindered by the "cold start" problem. According to their research, what is the recommended strategic approach for executives to overcome this barrier and drive institutional adoption?

    A) Issuing utility tokens alongside security tokens to artificially inflate daily trading volume and attract retail speculators.

    B) Assembling counterparties to collaborate and create a "minimum viable value chain" (MVVC) on common or interoperable networks.

    C) Abandoning permissioned networks entirely in favor of deploying all institutional assets on public, permissionless blockchains.

    D) Delaying all tokenization projects until a single, globally unified regulatory framework replaces all local jurisdictional rules.

    Question 6: IMF Research on CBDC Interoperability and Tokenization The IMF's research on cross-border payments identifies central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) as a critical enabler for tokenized asset settlement. According to IMF analysis, what is the primary bottleneck that wholesale CBDCs address in the current tokenized asset ecosystem?

    A) CBDCs address the lack of internet connectivity in developing markets, enabling all populations to participate in tokenized asset markets for the first time.

    B) Wholesale CBDCs provide the programmable, risk-free cash settlement leg for tokenized securities — resolving the current gap where the asset leg of a transaction can settle atomically on-chain, but the payment leg still relies on T+1/T+2 correspondent banking, preventing true atomic Delivery-versus-Payment.

    C) CBDCs primarily address cybersecurity vulnerabilities in existing blockchain protocols by providing central bank-encrypted transaction validation.

    D) The IMF recommends that all CBDCs replace existing cryptocurrencies and stablecoins, thereby standardizing tokenized asset settlement onto a single global payment infrastructure.

    Question 7: WEF Inclusive Deployment Framework The World Economic Forum's tokenization research emphasizes that the benefits of tokenized finance must be broadly distributed rather than exclusively capture by large incumbent institutions. What specific recommendation does the WEF make for ensuring inclusive deployment?

    A) The WEF recommends that all tokenization activity be restricted to public permissionless blockchains to prevent institutional monopolization of private network infrastructure.

    B) The WEF recommends that institutions actively design tokenization solutions that reduce barriers for previously excluded participants — including SME borrowers accessing tokenized trade finance, emerging market investors accessing global asset classes through fractionalization, and smaller asset managers accessing institutional-grade distribution infrastructure — rather than using tokenization solely to improve efficiency for already-dominant market participants.

    C) The WEF advises that tokenization should focus exclusively on developed market financial instruments, since emerging market regulatory environments are too fragmented to support compliant tokenized product launches.

    D) The WEF mandates that all tokenized products must be offered at zero management fees to democratize investment access, since the technology eliminates the cost basis that justifies traditional fund management fees.

    Question 8: Deloitte Digital Asset Custody Research Findings Deloitte's research on institutional digital asset custody identifies a specific gap between the custody solutions available for traditional securities and those currently available for tokenized assets. What is the most significant custody gap that institutions must address before scaling tokenized asset programs?

    A) Traditional custodians are technically incapable of storing digital private keys, making all tokenized asset custody structurally impossible for regulated institutions.

    B) While traditional securities custody is supported by decades of established legal precedent, bankruptcy-remote structures, and insurance frameworks, digital asset custody for tokenized securities lacks equivalent standardization — particularly regarding qualified custodian status under SEC Investment Advisers Act Rule 206(4)-2, insurance coverage for key management failures, and legally tested bankruptcy-remote legal structures for client assets held in digital wallets.

    C) Digital asset custody is exclusively available through offshore jurisdictions, preventing U.S. institutional asset managers from legally holding tokenized securities in compliant custody arrangements.

    D) Institutional digital asset custody solutions are already superior to traditional securities custody in all respects, making the transition from traditional to digital custody immediately cost-optimal.

    Question 9: BIS Annual Economic Report: Tokenization and Financial Stability The Bank for International Settlements' Annual Economic Report on tokenization identifies a positive and a negative scenario for systemic financial stability depending on how tokenization scales. What distinguishes the positive (stability-enhancing) scenario from the negative (risk-amplifying) scenario?

    A) The positive scenario requires all tokenized assets to operate on BIS-controlled ledgers; the negative scenario occurs when commercial banks control tokenized asset infrastructure.

    B) The positive scenario occurs when tokenization improves market transparency and atomic settlement, reducing systemic settlement risk and enabling superior real-time risk monitoring. The negative scenario occurs when tokenized products introduce new forms of liquidity-maturity mismatches, leverage, and interconnectedness that regulators cannot monitor using existing surveillance frameworks — amplifying rather than reducing financial instability.

    C) The BIS finds no distinction between positive and negative scenarios since tokenization's net impact on financial stability is mathematically neutral.

    D) The positive scenario requires that all tokenized assets be backed exclusively by government bonds; any other collateral type automatically triggers the negative stability scenario.

    Question 10: McKinsey Asset Management Research: Tokenization Cost Economics McKinsey's research on tokenization cost economics quantifies the operational cost savings available from tokenized fund administration compared to traditional structures. According to McKinsey, which specific cost component sees the greatest reduction from tokenization?

    A) Investment management fees (management fees and performance fees) are eliminated entirely through smart contract automation, reducing the all-in cost of fund investment by 80-90%.

    B) Compliance and reporting costs remain unchanged by tokenization, since regulatory obligations require human oversight regardless of the underlying settlement infrastructure.

    C) Tokenized funds see the greatest cost reduction in middle and back-office functions — including reconciliation, transfer agent operations, fund accounting, and corporate action processing — where smart contracts eliminate manual data entry, multi-system reconciliation, and the associated error-correction overhead.

    D) Tokenization reduces investment research costs by 50% through AI-generated trading signals embedded directly in smart contracts.

    Answer Key & Executive Explanations

    1. Answer: B * Explanation: BCG and Ripple's "Approaching the Tokenization Tipping Point" report projects an $18.9 trillion market by 2033, noting that the banking sector will account for over a third of all tokenized assets by the end of the decade, surging to over 50% by 2033.
